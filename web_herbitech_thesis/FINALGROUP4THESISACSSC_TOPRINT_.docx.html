ABSTRACT

This study introduces Herbitech, a mobile app created to help herbal plant growers in the Philippines detect plant diseases and monitor plant health using the YOLOv8 algorithm. It aims to improve traditional methods of checking plant health, which are often slow and inaccurate. Using artificial intelligence and image recognition, the app can tell if a plant is healthy or has a disease just by analyzing its photo. The researchers used a descriptive and developmental research approach, and the agile method, to build both the mobile app for staff and a web system for administrators. The model was trained using edited and improved plant images through Google Colab and Python, and showed good results in terms of accuracy and reliability. Key features of the app include scanning plant images for diseases, giving treatment suggestions, tracking plant health, managing plant records, and letting users contact experts. To check how well the app works, the team followed the ISO 25010 standards and tested it with 30 IT experts and 30 non-IT users. Results showed positive feedback on how useful, easy to use, and flexible the system is, with average scores of 3.75 for functionality, 3.71 for ease of interaction, and 3.81 for flexibility. To deal with issues like storage limits and relying only on visible symptoms, the app includes photo compression and lets users report cases to experts. Overall, Herbitech connects traditional plant care with modern AI, supports eco-friendly farming, and serves as a starting point for future research in smart plant care. herbal farming but also opens doors for future AI applications in agriculture.

CHAPTER I

INTRODUCTION

Herbal medicine has long been an integral part of Filipino culture, especially in rural communities where access to modern healthcare is limited. Herbal plants such as lagundi, sambong, and ampalaya are widely used for their natural healing properties and are often grown in backyard gardens and small-scale farms. However, the health and productivity of these plants are increasingly threatened by diseases that are difficult to detect through traditional methods. According to Gomez et al. (2022), the majority of herbal growers in the Philippines rely solely on manual observation to monitor plant health. This method is not only time-consuming but also prone to human error, which can result in delayed intervention, crop loss, and diminished medicinal quality.

In response to this challenge, the researchers developed Herbitech, a mobile application designed to support herbal growers in monitoring plant health through real-time disease detection. The app leverages artificial intelligence—specifically, computer vision and deep learning techniques—to analyze images of herbal plants and identify signs of disease. By simply capturing a photo of an affected plant, users can instantly receive information about possible infections and recommended management practices. Herbitech aims to empower growers with accurate, timely, and accessible information, reducing the need for chemical treatments and promoting sustainable agricultural practices. This initiative not only enhances plant health monitoring but also supports the preservation of herbal resources vital to Philippine traditional medicine.

Project Context

Herbal plants have long held an essential place in traditional medicine and continue to be integral to healthcare practices worldwide. Valued for their therapeutic properties, these plants support holistic health practices rooted in natural healing. In the Philippines, herbal medicine is particularly important, and its significance is underscored by initiatives such as the Department of Health's (DOH) "Herbal Medicinal Plants Project," which promotes the safe and effective use of indigenous plants with proven therapeutic effects. Notably, over 70% of Filipinos use herbal medicine to address common ailments, reflecting its accessibility and cultural importance (DOH, 2023).

In recent years, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in agriculture has gained significant attention for its potential to transform traditional farming practices. One critical area within this field is the early detection and management of plant diseases, a challenge that has long plagued agricultural productivity, particularly in regions like the Philippines where small-scale farmers are predominant. Studies, such as those conducted by Gomez et al. (2022), highlight the reliance on manual observation for identifying plant diseases—a method prone to inaccuracies and delays, often resulting in significant crop losses and reduced quality of produce. This issue is particularly relevant for herbal plants, which are deeply valued for their medicinal properties but face threats from undetected diseases that compromise their therapeutic efficacy.

This issue is especially critical for herbal plants, which are highly valued for their medicinal properties and play an essential role in traditional and modern medicine. These plants are cultivated for their healing compounds, which can have a profound impact on human health. However, when diseases go undetected, they not only threaten crop yields but also significantly compromise the therapeutic efficacy of these plants. The presence of unrecognized diseases can alter the plant's biochemical composition, rendering the medicinal properties ineffective and potentially harmful. Furthermore, the economic value of these plants is at risk, as growers and producers may face reduced yields.

The importance of timely disease detection and intervention in maintaining the health of herbal plants has been highlighted in recent studies. A 2023 study by Singh et al. found that early detection of plant diseases is crucial in maintaining the health and productivity of herbal plants, particularly for small-scale growers. The study demonstrated that plant diseases can severely affect herbal plant yields and quality, leading to economic losses for growers. It emphasized that adopting advanced disease detection technologies, such as AI-driven systems, can significantly improve plant health outcomes. In the context of the Philippines, where herbal plant production is vital for both local consumption and export, the application of real-time disease detection technologies is increasingly seen as essential. This is where technologies like the YOLOv8 object detection algorithm come into play. By applying YOLOv8, this system can rapidly capture and analyze images of herbal plants, providing immediate disease detection and management suggestions. This approach enables timely interventions, empowering herbal growers and organizations to take proactive measures, improve plant health, and adopt sustainable farming practices (Singh et al., 2023).

The process for analyzing plant diseases involves several key steps. First, data collection is conducted by gathering images of herbal plants exhibiting various health conditions. Next, data preprocessing is applied to prepare these images for model training using techniques such as resizing, normalization, and data augmentation. The YOLOv8 algorithm is then used to train the model on the processed dataset, enabling it to accurately detect and classify plant diseases. After training, the model undergoes evaluation using a separate validation dataset to assess its precision, recall, and overall performance. Once validated, the trained model is deployed into the mobile application, allowing users to capture or upload images of herbal plants. The system then performs object detection to identify the plant and analyze its health condition. If a disease is detected, the application provides relevant information about the condition and suggests possible remediation steps. Users can either implement the recommended solution or escalate the case to horticulturists via the web platform for further assessment. Plants with detected diseases are also added to a monitoring list until they are marked as recovered or decomposed, ensuring continuous plant health management and recordkeeping.

The integration of YOLOv8 into the mobile application enhances its functionality by offering a comprehensive approach to plant health management. First, the application identifies specific diseases affecting plants through advanced image analysis with at least 87% accuracy of disease detection, and provides corresponding recommendations. The application includes a description of the disease detected, recommendations and suggestions to cure the herbal plant disease. Furthermore, the application provides practical steps for managing the herbal plant disease, including treatment options, preventive measures, or recommendations to consult the organization’s horticulturist. By combining these features, the application equips users with the necessary tools needed to maintain plant health effectively and sustainably.

Purpose and Description of the Study

The purpose of this study is to enhance herbal plant health management through the development of a mobile application that empowers users to detect and understand plant diseases using the YOLOv8 algorithm. The application will deliver practical recommendations for disease management, helping users to take timely action to help preserve the medicinal quality and health of the herbal plants for herbal growers and organizations in the Philippines that handles the production of herbal plants. Through the integration of advanced technology and a user-friendly interface, the mobile application aspires to make plant disease detection accessible right at the fingertips for staff and horticulturist use. The algorithm is expected to detect the health of the herbal plant and classify herbal plant diseases by scanning the image. The core strategy of this solution involves the collection of a diverse dataset of herbal plants and plant diseases. In this context, an algorithm refers to a set of systematic, step-by-step instructions designed to analyze data, make decisions, and perform tasks automatically.

To ensure that the application addresses user needs effectively, the researchers conducted a survey using Google Forms, which gathered responses from 60 participants, split evenly between IT experts and non-IT experts to evaluate users’ familiarity with medicinal plants, common plant diseases, and their preferences for application features. The feedback gathered from this survey will inform the design and functionality of the application, with an emphasis on intuitive use and practical utility. This study ultimately aims to create a valuable tool that enhances users' ability to manage plant health autonomously while recognizing that expert knowledge remains indispensable in complex or severe cases. Through this study, the researchers aim to create a practical tool that assists users in managing plant health, offering guidance without replacing expert knowledge.

Scope and Limitations

The primary focus of this research is to design and develop the Herbitech, an application that utilizes YOLOv8 algorithm for herbal plants detection and herbal plant disease detection to monitor the health of herbal plants. The evaluation of YOLOv8 will be based on three key performance metrics: precision, recall, and accuracy. Precision refers to the proportion of correctly identified objects among all detections made by the model. At the same time, recall measures the model’s ability to detect all relevant objects present in the images. Accuracy, meanwhile, indicates the overall correctness of the model’s predictions. The assessment will be conducted in a simulation developed by the authors using Google Collab software in the Python programming language Version 3.10.

Limitations

While Herbitech offers advanced capabilities in disease detection, several limitations are inherent to the application. Furthermore, the application may not fully account for environmental factors affecting herbal plant health, such as soil quality, humidity, and temperature changes. The detection capabilities are primarily focused on visible symptoms of diseases and herbal plant diseases that have already been discovered; thus, Herbitech may struggle with non-visible diseases that are not immediately apparent through visual inspection. In addition, Herbitech may face limitations in identifying newly discovered herbal plant diseases that are not included in its training data. Since the model relies on pre-existing datasets for disease detection, any diseases that emerge after its development or are not well-documented may go unrecognized. The model's performance may be limited by the availability of annotated images of rare or newly emerging plant diseases, which could affect detection accuracy. To address this, we have developed a "report to expert" feature that allows staff to submit disease cases for diagnosis by horticulturists. This functionality supports expert guidance and contributes to creating a valuable dataset for future researchers, serving as a foundational resource for further advancements in herbal plant disease detection. Furthermore, Herbitech is limited to the visible parts and popular parts of herbal plants that are commonly used for medicinal benefits, such as leaves, and stems. Some roots of herbal plants may be included as it is commonly used for medicinal benefits. Another limitation is its dependence on a stable network connection, which is necessary for disease detection and data synchronization.

Herbitech relies on the YOLOv8 algorithm for image-based detection, which may sometimes misidentify herbal plant diseases due to varying environmental conditions, such as lighting, background, or image angles. The YOLOv8 architecture, which powers the disease detection component of the Herbitech mobile application, incorporates a specialized structure known as the C2f block. This component is fundamental to the model's capability to perform efficient and accurate object detection, particularly when applied to complex image recognition tasks such as identifying herbal plants and diagnosing visual disease symptoms.

The C2f block—short for “Concatenation of 2 branches with feature extraction”—represents a modular design pattern within the YOLOv8 framework. It consists of several key elements:

1. Input Layer

The architecture begins with an image input of dimensions h × w × c<sub>in</sub>, where h is the height, w is the width, and c<sub>in</sub> is the number of input channels (commonly 3 for RGB images). In the context of Herbitech, this input corresponds to the captured or uploaded image of an herbal plant from the mobile device.

2. Initial Convolution Layer

The first operation applied to the input is a 1×1 convolutional layer with a stride of 1 and no padding. This layer performs feature compression or expansion and serves to normalize the input before deeper processing. It enables the model to efficiently begin extracting spatial features—such as edges, colors, and textures—that are relevant for identifying disease symptoms on leaves, stems, or roots.

3. Split Operation

After the initial convolution, the feature map is split into two branches, facilitating parallel processing. This split allows the model to process distinct features simultaneously, increasing the richness of learned representations without a significant increase in computational cost. For plant disease detection, this helps differentiate between healthy and unhealthy tissue patterns by evaluating multiple visual cues in parallel.

4. Bottleneck Blocks with Residual Connections

Each branch passes through multiple Bottleneck blocks, repeated n times. These blocks combine standard convolutional operations with batch normalization and non-linear activation functions such as ReLU or SiLU. The Bottleneck design improves learning efficiency by reducing the number of parameters while maintaining expressiveness.

An essential enhancement in these blocks is the use of residual connections—denoted as shortcut connections. These connections allow the output of a layer to be added to its input, helping to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and enabling deeper training. In Herbitech, this ensures that the YOLOv8 model can detect subtle disease patterns in plants, even when trained on limited or noisy datasets.

5. Concatenation (Concat)

After feature extraction in each branch, the outputs are concatenated along the channel dimension. This step fuses diverse feature maps, enhancing the model’s capacity to represent complex patterns. In practical terms, this means that the application can more accurately interpret overlapping disease symptoms or variations in plant structure.

6. Final Convolution Layer

The concatenated features are processed by another convolutional layer to further refine the combined representations. This consolidates the extracted information and prepares it for high-level inference.

7. C2f Output and Feature Map

The final output of this structure is termed C2f, which refers to the refined feature map used by the YOLOv8 detection head to predict bounding boxes, class probabilities, and confidence scores. In the context of Herbitech, the C2f output directly contributes to determining whether the plant is healthy or afflicted by specific diseases (e.g., Rust or Powdery Mildew). Spatial Pyramid Pooling – Fast (SPPF)

The Spatial Pyramid Pooling – Fast (SPPF) module enables the model to extract features at multiple spatial resolutions. This is particularly important for object detection tasks in which targets (e.g., plant disease symptoms) can appear in varying sizes. The SPPF block in YOLOv8 is a computationally efficient adaptation of earlier spatial pyramid structures, aimed at reducing processing time while maintaining the model's capacity to detect features at different scales.

The SPPF process begins with a convolutional layer with a 3×3 kernel, stride of 2, and no padding (k=3, s=2, p=0), which downsamples the input feature map and initiates feature extraction. The output of this layer is passed through three consecutive MaxPool2D layers, each of which further downsamples the feature map and captures progressively larger receptive fields. The outputs from these pooling operations are then concatenated along the channel dimension, effectively merging multi-scale feature representations. A final convolutional layer (k=3, s=2, p=1) refines the combined output.

In the context of Herbitech, the SPPF module enables the system to detect plant diseases that vary in size and shape—such as localized fungal spots or large-scale leaf discolorations. By capturing multi-scale features, the model becomes more robust to variability in image capture conditions, including zoom levels and distances from the plant.

Convolutional Layers (Conv)

Convolutional layers (Conv) form the foundational building blocks of the YOLOv8 architecture. These modules are responsible for local feature extraction, dimensionality reduction or expansion, and transformation of the input feature maps. Each Conv block in YOLOv8 comprises the following layers:

  • Conv2D Layer: Applies a set of filters to the input to detect spatial patterns such as edges, textures, and gradients.
  • BatchNormalization2D: Normalizes the activations from the convolutional layer to stabilize training and accelerate convergence.
  • SiLU Activation Function: Introduces non-linearity and smooth gradient flow, improving the model’s ability to learn complex mappings.

In Herbitech, the Conv modules are instrumental in learning discriminative features from plant images, such as lesion boundaries, color irregularities, and shape deformations key indicators of herbal plant diseases like Rust or Powdery Mildew. These layers also contribute to generalizing the detection system to handle various herbal species under different lighting and background conditions.

Detect Head Architecture

The Detect head consists of a sequence of convolutional operations that transform high-dimensional feature representations into interpretable detection results. Its structure includes the following key components:

Input Feature Map

The Detect head receives a feature map as input from the previous layers of the YOLOv8 network, which includes modules such as the C2f block and the Spatial Pyramid Pooling – Fast (SPPF). These feature maps contain enriched spatial and semantic information crucial for identifying detailed patterns associated with plant diseases.

Double Convolutional Layers

The input feature map is processed through two consecutive convolutional layers, each utilizing a kernel size of 3, stride of 1, and padding of 1 (k=3, s=1, p=1). These layers refine the extracted features by enhancing their discriminative power for object detection. In the case of Herbitech, this enables more precise recognition of disease symptoms such as leaf discoloration, fungal patches, or texture changes.

Dual Output Conv2D Layers

After feature refinement, the data is passed into two parallel Conv2D layers, each responsible for a specific type of prediction:

  • Bounding Box Prediction Layer (Bbox Head)
  • This layer outputs c = 4 × reg_max values, where 4 corresponds to the bounding box coordinates (x, y, width, height), and reg_max is a hyperparameter related to the regression resolution. The output is evaluated through a Bounding Box Loss function, which minimizes the error between predicted and ground-truth object locations.
  • Class Prediction Layer (Cls Head)
  • This layer outputs c = nc channels, where nc represents the number of object classes. Loss Functions and Model Optimization

The model is optimized through a combined loss function that includes both localization loss (bounding box error) and classification loss (class prediction error). These loss components guide the model during training to reduce discrepancies between predictions and annotated labels in the dataset.

Bounding Box Loss ensures accurate object localization by penalizing incorrectly sized or misplaced bounding boxes. Classification Loss ensures correct categorization by comparing predicted class probabilities with actual class labels. These losses are minimized using backpropagation and gradient descent techniques, allowing the network to continuously improve its detection accuracy over successive training epochs.

Accuracy quantifies the overall correctness of the model's predictions. It is calculated as the ratio of correctly classified instances (both true positives and true negatives) to the total number of instances in the dataset. A high accuracy rate suggests that the model is generally performing well, correctly classifying a significant proportion of the data points. Accuracy can sometimes present an incomplete picture of a model’s performance, especially when working with imbalanced datasets where one class has significantly more samples than others. In these situations, a model may achieve a high accuracy rate by consistently predicting the dominant class while failing to correctly identify instances from the minority class. This highlights the importance of using more comprehensive evaluation metrics to better assess the model’s effectiveness across all classes.

Precision, in contrast to accuracy, focuses specifically on the accuracy of positive predictions. It measures the proportion of true positives (correctly identified positive instances) among all instances predicted as positive (including both true positives and false positives). A high precision score indicates that the model is reliable in its positive predictions, minimizing the occurrence of false positives (incorrectly identifying negative instances as positive). Precision is particularly important in applications where false positives are costly or undesirable, such as medical diagnosis or fraud detection.

Recall offers a different perspective by focusing on the model's ability to identify all actual positive instances. It measures the proportion of true positives among all actual positive instances in the dataset (including both true positives and false negatives). A high recall score indicates that the model is effectively capturing all positive instances, minimizing the occurrence of false negatives (incorrectly identifying positive instances as negative). Recall is crucial in applications where missing positive instances are highly problematic, such as disease detection or spam filtering, where failing to identify a positive instance can have significant consequences.

The F1 Score provides a balanced measure of a model’s performance by combining both precision and recall into a single metric. It is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and is particularly useful when there is an uneven class distribution or when both false positives and false negatives are significant. A high F1 Score indicates that the model performs well in terms of both accurately identifying positive instances and capturing most of them. It is considered a more reliable indicator of model performance than accuracy alone in complex or imbalanced classification tasks, such as plant disease detection where every correctly identified case matters.

Figure 4.1 Recall Confidence Curve

Figure 4.1 illustrates the Recall Confidence Curve, which is an important diagnostic tool used to assess the relationship between the model's confidence in its predictions and its ability to correctly identify true positives (recall). In the context of the Herbitech application, this curve was used to evaluate how effectively the YOLOv8 model detects various herbal plant diseases across different confidence score thresholds. In object detection models like YOLOv8, each detection is associated with a confidence score—a probability indicating how certain the model is about the predicted class. The Recall Confidence Curve plots the recall value (y-axis) against a range of confidence thresholds (x-axis), allowing researchers to understand how the model performs as its confidence requirement increases or decreases.

Understanding the Curve

  • High Recall at Low Confidence: At lower confidence thresholds (e.g., 0.2 to 0.4), the model casts a wider net, detecting more potential instances of disease, including borderline cases. This results in high recall, as most true positives are included—but at the cost of increasing false positives.
  • Decline in Recall with Higher Confidence Thresholds: As the confidence threshold increases (e.g., beyond 0.7), the model becomes stricter, only making predictions when it is very sure. While this reduces false positives, it may also miss less obvious cases, causing recall to drop. This trade-off highlights the balance between sensitivity (recall) and specificity (precision).

At each confidence threshold level, recall is recalculated by counting only the predictions that meet or exceed that confidence score.In the Herbitech project, the recall confidence curve demonstrates that the YOLOv8 model maintains high recall (> 0.85) across a wide range of lower to mid-range confidence thresholds (from 0.3 to 0.6). This means the model is generally effective at identifying most diseased instances, even at moderate confidence levels.

The curve shows a gradual decline in recall as the threshold exceeds 0.7, eventually stabilizing below 0.70 at the 0.9 threshold. This trend suggests that while the model becomes more conservative in its predictions at higher thresholds, it starts missing a significant portion of actual disease instances. Therefore, for practical deployment, a confidence threshold around 0.5 to 0.6 was identified as optimal—balancing both high recall and acceptable precision.

This insight is particularly valuable for use cases in real-world agricultural settings, where failing to detect a disease early can lead to significant yield losses. By tuning the confidence threshold to maximize recall without overwhelming users with false positives, Herbitech ensures more reliable plant health monitoring and more actionable results for users.

To evaluate the non-functional performance of the Herbitech system, we conducted a series of tests using Apache JMeter, an open-source tool widely utilized in both academic research and industry for load simulation and system performance analysis. Our objective was to verify that the application remains stable, responsive, and efficient when subjected to concurrent user interactions and heavy data operations—conditions that simulate real-world use cases in the field.

Test Environment Setup

All tests were executed in a controlled environment using our development server and production-ready builds of the Herbitech mobile and web applications. We used Apache JMeter version 5.6.2 and configured it with various components such as thread groups, HTTP samplers, listeners, and assertions to accurately replicate end-user behavior.

The test plan we designed included the following:

  • Thread Groups: We simulated 300 concurrent users per module, allowing us to evaluate how the system handles high-traffic loads.
  • Loop Count: Each module was tested with 300 samples, resulting in a total of 6,300 requests across the system.
  • Ramp-Up Time: We configured a gradual increase in user threads to mimic natural usage and prevent artificial system stress.
  • Timers: To replicate real user delays between actions, we implemented think time and uniform random timers.
  • HTTP Requests: Each sampler corresponded to a specific module (e.g., LoginPage, UpdatePlantMonitoring, ReadReport), and was set up with the appropriate endpoints, request payloads, and headers.

Metrics and Data Collection

During the execution of the test, we collected and analyzed the following performance metrics:

  • Average Response Time – to determine how quickly the system responds under simulated load conditions.
  • Maximum Response Time – to identify peak latency across modules and assess worst-case performance scenarios.
  • Error Rate – to ensure system reliability and verify that no requests failed during the simulation.
  • Throughput (Requests per Second) – to evaluate the rate at which the system can handle incoming requests.
  • Data Throughput (KB/sec) – to measure the volume of inbound (received) and outbound (sent) traffic and ensure optimal resource usage.
  • Standard Deviation – to assess the consistency of response times, particularly in modules that involve dynamic content or machine learning tasks.

We individually tested each module using its corresponding JMeter sampler. Modules such as UpdatePlantMonitoring and ReportAnalysis were considered high-load due to the inclusion of image uploads, data processing, and YOLOv8-based detection logic. To simulate realistic backend activity, we incorporated JSON payloads, image files, and triggered Firebase Cloud Functions where applicable.

Tools and Utilities Used

In addition to JMeter, we utilized the following tools to support our testing process:

  • Firebase Monitoring Dashboard – for tracking backend usage, storage, and real-time resource consumption.
  • Postman – for verifying all API endpoints before integrating them into JMeter scripts.
  • JSON Extractors and Assertions – for validating API responses and ensuring data integrity.
  • Custom Automation Scripts – to handle session tokens, login credentials, and simulate user workflows including authentication and authorization.

A total of 6,300 samples were generated, simulating 300 concurrent requests per module. This test covered core functionalities such as login, scanning, updating plant monitoring records, and generating reports.

  • The average response times varied significantly depending on the complexity of the operation. For instance, the LoginPage averaged 1,223 ms, which is ideal for a lightweight authentication task.   • The maximum response time observed was 36,106 ms in the UpdatePlantMonitoring module, which reflects the intensive computational nature of that functionality. Despite this, the system maintained 0.00% error rate across all modules, indicating robust reliability and the system’s ability to maintain service quality under load without critical failures.

2. Throughput Test Analysis

Throughput results reflect how many requests per second the system could process:

  • The system achieved a high throughput of 118.5 requests/sec on the LoginPage module, which demonstrates excellent performance for lightweight operations.
  • For heavier operations like UpdatePlantMonitoring, the throughput dipped to 9.7 requests/sec, which is still reasonable considering the nature of operations being conducted (e.g., image uploads, disease detection, monitoring record updates).
  • The overall average throughput across all modules was 32.3 requests/sec, surpassing the target benchmark of 30 requests/sec. This validates the system's capacity to scale across various concurrent tasks without significant performance degradation.

3. Stress Test

To evaluate the system’s stability and performance under extreme operational conditions, a stress test was conducted focusing on data handling and response behavior during high-load scenarios:

  • The average incoming data rate was recorded at 601.41 KB/sec, peaking at 1,403.25 KB/sec in the UpdatePlantMonitoring module.   • The average outgoing data rate was relatively low at 4.2 KB/sec, suggesting that the system is optimized for upload-heavy workflows while minimizing outbound transmission—an essential factor for users in bandwidth-constrained environments.
  • The PlantInfo and ReadReport modules showed high average bytes per request, measuring 140,496.9 bytes and 134,508.4 bytes respectively. These values are consistent with modules that process large files such as plant images, comprehensive diagnostic reports, and historical records.
  • A standard deviation of 7,212.31 ms in response times for UpdatePlantMonitoring reflects expected variability in modules involving asynchronous tasks, such as real-time inference using trained machine learning models or rendering complex plant data.

This stress test confirms the Herbitech system’s ability to handle data-intensive operations and maintain operational integrity under peak usage condition

Overall, the evaluation results confirm the application's effectiveness, usability, and adaptability, with consistently high ratings across all categories. Both IT experts and non-IT users expressed strong agreement on the system's quality, reinforcing its potential for continued improvement and wider adoption. While minor areas for refinement were identified, the application is well-received and holds promise for future development and expansion.

Conclusion

The study successfully developed and evaluated Herbitech, a mobile application designed for herbal plant disease detection using the YOLOv8 object detection algorithm. Through a thorough examination of plant monitoring and disease detection methods, it was found that traditional approaches were inefficient, emphasizing the need for AI-driven solutions. The research explored existing applications and identified key gaps, such as the lack of detection and integrated treatment recommendations, which were addressed in the development of Herbitech. The application was designed with essential features for both users, and administrators, including plant scanning, health monitoring, inventory management, report generation, user account management, and remediation. Evaluation based on ISO 25010 criteria confirmed that the application met standards of functional suitability, interaction capability, compatibility, and maintainability, making it a reliable tool for herbal plant health management. While the application demonstrated strong performance and usability, areas for improvement were identified, such as expanding the disease dataset for enhanced accuracy and optimizing the user interface for a more seamless experience. Overall, Herbitech proves to be an innovative and effective solution that empowers users to monitor plant health efficiently, facilitating early disease detection and promoting sustainable plant care practices.

class HerbitechApp extends StatelessWidget {

  @override

  Widget build(BuildContext context) {

      title: 'Herbitech Detection',

      theme: ThemeData(primarySwatch: Colors.green),

    );

}

class DetectionScreen extends StatefulWidget {

  @override

  _DetectionScreenState createState() => _DetectionScreenState();

}

class _DetectionScreenState extends State<DetectionScreen> {

  Interpreter? _interpreter;

  File? _image;

  final picker = ImagePicker();

  List<Detection>? _results;

  @override

  void initState() {

    super.initState();

    loadModel();

  }

  // STEP 1: Load YOLOv8 model

  Future<void> loadModel() async {

    _interpreter = await Interpreter.fromAsset('assets/model_unquant.tflite');

  }

  // STEP 2: Pick image from gallery

  Future<void> pickImage() async {

    final pickedFile = await picker.pickImage(source: ImageSource.gallery);

    if (pickedFile != null) {

      setState(() {

        _image = File(pickedFile.path);

      });

      await detectDisease(_image!);

    }

  }

  // STEP 3: Preprocess image and run inference

  Future<void> detectDisease(File imageFile) async {

    if (_interpreter == null) return;

    // 3.1 Decode image

    final originalImage = img.decodeImage(imageFile.readAsBytesSync())!;

    // 3.2 Resize image to YOLOv8 input size (640x640)

    final resized = img.copyResize(originalImage, width: 640, height: 640);

    // 3.3 Normalize and convert image to byte list

    var input = imageToByteList(resized);

    // 3.4 Prepare output tensor

    var output = List.generate(1, (i) => List.filled(25200, 0.0));

    // 3.5 Run inference

    _interpreter!.run(input, output);

    // STEP 4: Post-processing

    var detections = processOutput(output[0]);

    setState(() {

      _results = detections;

    });

  }

  // STEP 3.3: Convert image to normalized byte list

  Uint8List imageToByteList(img.Image image) {

    var bytes = Uint8List(1 * 640 * 640 * 3);

    var buffer = ByteData.view(bytes.buffer);

    int idx = 0;

    for (int y = 0; y < 640; y++) {

      for (int x = 0; x < 640; x++) {

        int pixel = image.getPixel(x, y);

        buffer.setUint8(idx++, (pixel >> 16) & 0xFF);

        buffer.setUint8(idx++, (pixel >> 8) & 0xFF);

        buffer.setUint8(idx++, pixel & 0xFF);

      }

    }

    return bytes;

  }

  // STEP 4: Decode YOLOv8 output and apply Non-Maximum Suppression

  List<Detection> processOutput(List<double> output) {

    var rawBoxes = decodeBoxes(output);

    var finalDetections = applyNMS(rawBoxes);

    return finalDetections;

  }

  // STEP 4.1: Decode raw bounding boxes

  List<Detection> decodeBoxes(List<double> output) {

    List<Detection> detections = [];

    for (int i = 0; i < output.length; i += 5) {

      double x = output[i];

      double y = output[i + 1];

      double w = output[i + 2];

      double h = output[i + 3];

      double confidence = output[i + 4];

      if (confidence > 0.5) {

        Rect box = Rect.fromCenter(center: Offset(x, y), width: w, height: h);

        detections.add(Detection("Herbal Disease", confidence, box));

      }

    }

    return detections;

  }

  // STEP 4.2: Apply Non-Maximum Suppression to reduce overlapping boxes

  List<Detection> applyNMS(List<Detection> detections) {

    // For simplicity, returns all high-confidence detections.

    // Advanced logic such as Soft-NMS or IoU-based filtering can be added.

    return detections;

  }

  @override

  void dispose() {

    _interpreter?.close();

    super.dispose();

  }

  @override

  Widget build(BuildContext context) {

    return Scaffold(

      appBar: AppBar(title: Text("Herbitech Plant Scanner")),

      body: Center(

        child: SingleChildScrollView(

          child: Column(

            mainAxisAlignment: MainAxisAlignment.center,

            children: [

              _image == null

                  ? Text("No image selected")

                  : Image.file(_image!),

              SizedBox(height: 20),

              ElevatedButton(

                onPressed: pickImage,

                child: Text("Select Image for Detection"),

              ),

              SizedBox(height: 20),

              if (_results != null)

                ..._results!.map((d) => Text(

                    "Detected: ${d.className} (Confidence: ${d.confidence.toStringAsFixed(2)})")),

            ],

          ),

        ),

      ),

    );

  }

}
